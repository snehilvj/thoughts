People ask me this a lot. Usually I give the polished answer — something about solving problems, making an impact, building something meaningful. And that's true, but it's not the whole truth.

The whole truth is messier.

## The comfortable trap

I had a good engineering job. Good pay, interesting problems, smart colleagues. The kind of setup most people would be happy with. And I was happy — for a while.

But there's this thing that happens when you're a builder by nature. You start seeing problems everywhere. Not in a cynical way, but in a "I could fix that" way. And when you see enough problems that nobody seems to be fixing, the itch becomes unbearable.

## What nobody tells you

Here's what the startup Twitter crowd doesn't mention:

1. **The loneliness is real.** When you're a founding engineer, there's no one to rubber-duck with at 2 AM when your architecture decision might be wrong.

2. **You'll question everything.** Not just your product decisions, but your life decisions. Weekly.

3. **The financial anxiety doesn't go away.** Even when things are going well, your brain keeps a running "months of runway" counter.

4. **You'll become a worse engineer.** Not because your skills atrophy, but because you start optimizing for "good enough to ship" instead of "elegant and correct." And honestly, that's the right call.

> The best code I've ever written is code I didn't write at all — a third-party integration that solved a problem in 20 minutes instead of the two weeks I'd have spent building it myself.

## So why do it?

Because the alternative was wondering "what if" for the rest of my career. And that felt worse than any of the downsides.

There's a particular flavor of satisfaction that comes from seeing someone use something you built — not your employer's product, but *your* thing. It's addictive in the best way.

## The uncomfortable truth

I also did it because I'm stubborn, slightly delusional about my own abilities, and bad at being told what to work on. These aren't virtues in most contexts, but they happen to be useful traits for a founder.

Not every reason has to be noble. Sometimes you start a startup because you're constitutionally incapable of not doing so. And that's okay.

---

*If you're thinking about starting something, my advice is simple: talk to people who've done it. Not the success stories on podcasts, but the ones in the trenches. Buy them coffee. Ask the uncomfortable questions. Then decide.*
